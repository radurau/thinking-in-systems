Discipline

Don't maximize effort. Distribute it.

Limiting Factors

Little by little helps. Excess kills.

Liebig's law: a plant grows with the nutrient it is short of. Keep adding one it already has, and first nothing happens — then harm.

Reinforcing loop — comfort feeds routine Success to the successful — skill attracts resources Limiting factor — Liebig's law Commons — attention is finite

Once a part is no longer the constraint, more effort there is waste — and past a point, damage.

Eight recurring structures. No villains required.

Policy resistance

Fixes that fail. Everyone pushes, the system pushes back — the quick fix relieves the symptom while quietly feeding the problem.

Romania, 1967. To force the birth rate up, Ceaușescu abruptly banned abortion and contraception. Births tripled — then the people pushed back. The rate fell again through dangerous illegal abortions (maternal deaths tripled), and unwanted children filled the orphanages. When the regime fell, the first law repealed was the ban.

Closer to home. Every “no-meetings Wednesday” rule: the meetings move to Tuesday and Thursday. The pressure that created them is untouched.

The trap

Different actors haul one stock toward different goals. Every new push just drags it farther from someone else's goal — so everyone burns energy and no one likes the result.

The way out

Stop pushing harder. Bring all the actors in and find a larger goal they'll pull toward willingly — as Sweden did, aligning on child welfare, not family size.

Tragedy of the commons

Everyone acts sensibly — the shared thing dies anyway. The meeting-room calendar. The oceans. The on-call rota.

Back to our fishery. Every boat keeps the whole gain from one more net, but shares the cost of a thinner sea with every other boat. So every sensible captain casts one more — and the stock that was flow-limited becomes stock-limited: the sea turns into an oil field. Same shape: crowded parks, a warming atmosphere.

Closer to home. Compute is the shared sea. Every model, pipeline and agent takes a little more RAM, CPU and bandwidth than the last — hardware grew 100,000× and we are still short.

The trap

A shared resource gives each user the full benefit of use but only a sliver of the cost of abuse. The feedback is too weak — so it's overused until it collapses.

The way out

Educate and appeal to conscience — then restore the missing feedback: privatize so each user feels their own impact, or regulate access ("mutual coercion, mutually agreed upon").

Drift to low performance

Bad becomes normal one small excuse at a time. Nobody decided to have flaky tests — the standard just… drifted.

The boiled frog. Every time results dip, you believe the bad news and quietly lower the bar — "not much worse than last year." The standard erodes to match, effort drops, performance sinks further. Meadows watched her own jogging habit fade out exactly this way.

Closer to home. An alert fires so often that nobody reads it — so we raise the threshold instead of fixing the cause. Then again. The boiled frog wears a pager.

The trap

When the standard is set by recent — and pessimistically perceived — performance, a reinforcing loop of eroding goals drags the system steadily downhill.

The way out

Hold the standard absolute. Better still, peg it to your best past performance, not your worst — and the same loop pulls you upward.

Escalation

"I'll raise you one." Price wars, feature wars, the reply-all thread. It's a reinforcing loop — it compounds.

"I'll raise you one." Kids trading harder hits; the US–USSR arms race, each side inflating the other's arsenal to justify its own — trillions spent, doomsday weapons built. Price wars and attack-ad spirals run on the same engine.

Closer to home. A colleague ships twice as fast with an AI assistant; you feel you must too, then more. Soon everyone is micro-maxing output and nobody can review it. The exit is to step down unilaterally — or to ask first: am I in a trap?

The trap

Each player's goal is simply to beat the other's current state. That's a reinforcing loop — it grows exponentially and ends in someone's collapse.

The way out

Best, don't enter. If you're in it: unilaterally step down to break the loop, or negotiate balancing limits both sides agree to.

Success to the successful

Winners get the resources to keep winning. The strong team gets the best projects — which makes it stronger.

Monopoly. The first player to build hotels collects rent, buys more hotels, and ends up owning the board. Ecologists call it competitive exclusion; in markets it shrank US carmakers to three and most cities to one newspaper. Winners keep winning; losers get squeezed out.

Closer to home. The team that shipped last quarter gets the headcount, so it ships more, so it gets more. Ask what the other teams would have done with the same slack.

The trap

When winning is rewarded with the means to win again, a reinforcing loop lets the winners take all while the losers are eliminated.

The way out

Diversify into a new game; cap any one winner (antitrust); or level the playing field — handicaps, progressive and inheritance taxes, the potlatch — so each round starts fairer.

Shifting the burden

The workaround works, so the real fix never gets built. Meadows' word for this one is addiction. Every "temporary" process is five years old.

Addiction. A drug numbs the pain without fixing what's wrong — so the real problem festers and you need more. Same shape beyond people: industries hooked on subsidies, farms on fertilizer, economies on cheap oil. The crutch erodes the system's own ability to cope.

Closer to home. Vibe-coding. Let the assistant fix it now, and you slowly lose the ability to fix it yourself — the way the calculator ate mental arithmetic. Help that breeds dependency is the intervenor’s trap.

The trap

A fix relieves the symptom, not the cause, and quietly atrophies the system's capacity to help itself — so it needs ever more of the fix.

The way out

Best, avoid the easy fix. If you're hooked, shift focus to long-term restructuring — rebuild the system's own capability, then remove the prop.

Rule beating

Obeying the letter of the rule while defeating its purpose. Hitting the metric, missing the point.

Letter, not spirit. Departments blow the budget every December so next year's isn't cut; Vermont's 10-acre rule bred a rash of 10.1-acre lots; some landowners quietly killed an endangered species so their land stayed developable.

Closer to home. The department that spends its whole budget in December, because next year’s budget is this year’s spend. Drivers who slow for the camera, not for the curve.

The trap

Rules invite perverse workarounds that look like compliance while distorting the system.

The way out

Read rule-beating as feedback. Redesign the rule so the system's cleverness serves its purpose instead of evading it.

Seeking the wrong goal

You get what you measure — exactly that, and nothing more. The big target was the point; the small one was just easier to hit.

You get what you measure. Define security as military spending and you get spending, not safety. Grade schools by test scores and you get test scores. India once counted IUDs implanted — so doctors inserted them without consent. Chase GNP and you count car crashes as growth.

Closer to home. Measure effort instead of results and the boat gets optimised for speed until it no longer looks like a boat — velocity charts that climb while customers leave.

The trap

If the goal or indicator is defined badly, the system obediently produces the wrong result — and looks busy doing it.

The way out

Choose indicators that reflect the system's real welfare, and never confuse effort with result.
